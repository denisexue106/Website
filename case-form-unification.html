01 · Why this work

Seven ways to build a form, and users still left

Four live form products and three legacy ones, each with its own UX, spread across four owning teams. Mine owned four of them. Three other teams owned one each. Every improvement got rebuilt per builder or shipped to just one. Nobody owned the whole problem because it was not any one team’s. I took it on.

I thought web forms ARE public forms. Do not know the difference.Survey respondent

Users could not tell them apart

The naming was the product’s vocabulary, not theirs. Legacy migration stalled for the same reason: with three modern builders, there was no obvious one to move to.

So they built forms elsewhere

Four rival form tools showed up in our own survey. One general-purpose tool alone was named by a quarter of respondents.

One form had got worse

I pulled every form’s measured score before drawing anything. Only one hit its target, and the most-used form scored lower after a rebuild than before it.

02 · My role

End-to-end design leadership

I was the only designer on the initiative. I framed it, ran the research, designed it, sold it and oversaw delivery.

01

Connected six years of orphaned research

Studies going back to 2020 each answered one question and then went quiet. Nobody had put them next to each other. Five prior studies are the reason I could argue this was structural and not a fresh opinion.

02

Ran the primary research

A 25-person survey with two researchers, a nine-platform competitor audit, a prioritization workshop, and I designed both usability studies.

03

Defined the north star and phasing

Parity first, consolidation second, appointments and payments after. Checkout belonged to another team, so the phasing was also a buy-in problem. I had to win that team before I could design into their surface.

04

Argued against the full rebuild

A rebuild was the assumed answer, and engineering estimated it at twelve-plus months. I showed the data models were already sound and the fragmentation was a surface problem, so a rebuild bought regression risk for marginal value. Winning that framing set the scope of everything after.

03 · The reframe

Ask what the form is for

Seven products became one builder and four use cases. The categorization is not cosmetic: it is what tells the system where a form is allowed to appear.

04 · Testing

Round one went badly, and that was useful

Round one · 7 participants

Every participant hit friction. Seven of seven could not find how to add a step, and six of seven stumbled on inherited taxonomy. The live side-by-side preview was named most often as the thing that built trust.

Round two · 8 participants

Manual creation scored a perfect 3.00 on the flow that had broken for everyone a year earlier. Most participants never noticed the public/internal split had been removed at all.

The finding that stuck

Nobody could find Forms. They looked in Automations, Contacts, Sales and Reporting first. The merge was invisible. The front door was not.

05 · AI

One target, one complete draft

Consolidation is also what made AI generation possible. One builder means one generation target, so a model can emit a complete form with full feature support, which no fragmented system could have offered.

Fields, copy, the brand from the Branding Center and a template arrive together, and the look changes without touching the fields. Recorded from the interactive prototype.

I won’t use AI for minor edits: typing instructions takes longer than just doing it. But I would for an entirely new form.Usability study participant

Testing was blunt: nearly everyone tries AI once, so the first output is the whole product. The cold start is designed, suggestion chips and a template fallback, so the first interaction is a nudge rather than an empty prompt box. Try the AI flow in the prototype →

06 · Judgment

Where I changed direction

The full rebuild
I unified the surface, preserved the foundation and staged the delivery.
Merging all form types at once
The backend was still genuinely split and checkout carried payment logic the others did not. I merged the two closest and designed the rest in as future states.
Full AI form creation
Testing said users did not want full automation: they want a starter, not an editor. I shipped generation with manual refinement, plus templates as the fallback.
Selling it with a deck
Slides could not make leadership feel a three-year destination. I built a working prototype instead. The “aha” landed in days, and I got the sign-off.

A Kano study on the three candidate features did not match the plan we had already written. Conditional logic was not on the roadmap at all, even though it was the top complaint in the survey and the top-ranked feature in the study. I took the results to leadership with the evidence behind them and changed the roadmap: conditional logic moved up, and the payments phase came out.

07 · Outcome

What shipped, and what ships next

ShippedAll three of the survey’s top-ranked parity features, live to users.
ShippedThe first half of the unified builder. It ships in stages, and the second half is in development.
In progressOne independently deployable form service replacing 32 legacy endpoints, twelve addressed, the remaining gaps mapped.
UnfundedFurther build-out was scoped and did not get funded.

The consolidated service needs one team, and the tech debt and maintenance cost of seven separate implementations goes with it. Consolidation was only half of it: forms are now integrated across every area of the platform rather than sitting inside one product, so a form can be used wherever the work actually happens. Landing that means working across four teams, and the system now carries AI and MCP layers on top of the unified foundation. The migration is running well so far: 79% moved across seamlessly, with support tickets down and no repeat maintenance on the same issues.

I would rather show this honestly than pretend it all landed. It ships in stages, and the order is the argument: the stages that went first are the ones a ranked user need could justify.

One thing I would do differently

I fixed the builder before the front door
The single most repeated finding in testing was that nobody could find Forms at all. I treated navigation as a follow-on because parity features were what the survey ranked. I would sequence it the other way now: a builder nobody can find does not get used, however good it is, and the fix was far cheaper than the features I shipped ahead of it.
