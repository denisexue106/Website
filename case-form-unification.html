Case study · Forms platform

Merging seven form
builders into one

Keap had seven form builders that all did the same job, and no team owned the whole problem. I was the only designer on it. I did the research, designed the new builder, and used what I learned to change the roadmap. The first half of the new builder has shipped, and the second half is in development.

01 · The problem

Seven ways to build a form, and users still left

There were four live form products and three legacy ones, each with its own UX. My team owned four of them, and three other teams owned one each. Any improvement had to be rebuilt for every builder, or it only reached one. No single team owned the whole problem, so I took it on.

I thought web forms ARE public forms. Do not know the difference.Survey respondent

Users couldn’t tell them apart

The names came from our product vocabulary, not theirs. It’s also why the legacy migration stalled. With three modern builders, there was no obvious one to move to.

So they built forms elsewhere

Our own survey turned up four other form tools people were using instead. A quarter of respondents named the same general purpose tool.

One form got worse

Before drawing anything, I pulled the measured score for every form. Only one hit its target, and the most used form scored lower after its rebuild than before.

02 · My role

The only designer on it

I took it from scoping and research through design, the pitch to leadership, and delivery.

01

Pulled six years of old research together

There were studies going back to 2020. Each one answered a single question and then sat in a folder, and nobody had put them side by side. Those five earlier studies are what let me argue the problem was structural.

02

Ran new research

A survey of 25 people that I ran with two researchers, a competitor audit of nine platforms and a prioritization workshop. I also designed both usability studies.

03

Set the direction and the phases

Parity first, then consolidation, then appointments and payments. Checkout belonged to another team, so I had to get that team on board before I could design anything in their part of the product.

04

Argued against the full rebuild

Everyone assumed we’d rebuild, and engineering estimated that at twelve months or more. I showed that the data models were already sound and the fragmentation was in what users saw, so a rebuild would add regression risk without much payoff. Once I won that argument, it set the scope for everything after.

03 · The approach

Ask what the form is for

I designed one builder with four use cases to replace seven products. The use case you pick also decides where the form is allowed to appear in the product.

04 · Testing

Round one went badly, and that was useful

Round one · 7 participants

Everyone hit friction. None of the seven could figure out how to add a step, and six of seven got tripped up by the old categories. The live side by side preview was what people mentioned most when they talked about trusting it.

Round two · 8 participants

A year later, manual creation scored a perfect 3.00 on the same flow that had broken for everyone. Most people didn’t even notice the public/internal split was gone.

What still didn’t work

Finding Forms was the only real friction left. People looked in Automations, Contacts, Sales and Reporting first. The merge itself mostly went unnoticed, which was good, but getting to Forms was still a problem.

05 · AI

One builder made AI drafts possible

Having one builder is also what made AI generation work. With seven builders, a model would have had seven targets, each with different features. With one, it can produce a complete form that uses everything the builder supports.

The fields, copy, brand and template all come in together, and switching templates changes the look without touching the fields. Recorded from the interactive prototype.

I won’t use AI for minor edits: typing instructions takes longer than just doing it. But I would for an entirely new form.Usability study participant

Testing showed that nearly everyone tries AI once, so the first result has to be good. I added suggestion chips and templates to the starting screen, so nobody starts from an empty prompt box.

06 · Mobile

Built on a phone, filled on a phone

A form has two people holding a phone: the owner who makes it and the customer who fills it out. I designed the mobile flow for both, with the same builder, the same Thryv AI and the same four templates as desktop.

On the owner’s side, Thryv AI becomes a chat with answers you can tap, the draft arrives with the brand already on it, and sharing ends in a sheet. Recorded from the interactive prototype, on demo data.

Tap, don’t type

Typing is the slow part on a phone, so Thryv AI asks its two questions with answers you can tap. A full draft can come from two taps instead of a paragraph.

Actions in thumb reach

Publish and share sits in a bar at the bottom of the builder. Sharing opens a sheet with text, email and a QR code instead of sending people to another page.

Fields sized for fingers

Fields are 52 pixels tall with 16 pixel text, so iOS doesn’t zoom in. Each one opens the right keyboard, labels float up instead of vanishing, and Submit stays pinned in reach.

07 · Decisions

Where I changed direction

The full rebuild
I kept the existing backend, merged the UI on top of it, and shipped it in stages.
Merging all form types at once
The backend was still split, and checkout had payment logic the others didn’t. I merged the two closest first and designed the rest as future states.
Full AI form creation
Testing showed people didn’t want AI for everything. They wanted it to start a new form, not to make small edits. I shipped AI drafting with manual editing after it, and templates as the fallback.
Selling it with a deck
Slides weren’t going to show leadership what a three year plan would feel like to use. I built a working prototype instead. Leadership got it within days, and I got the sign off.

A Kano study on the three candidate features didn’t match the plan we’d already written. Conditional logic wasn’t on the roadmap at all, even though the survey ranked it highest among the features we didn’t already have, and it was the top ranked feature in the Kano study. I took the results to leadership and got the roadmap changed: conditional logic moved up, and the payments phase came out.

08 · Outcome

What shipped and what’s next

ShippedAll three of the survey’s top ranked parity features, live to users.
ShippedThe first half of the unified builder. It ships in stages, and the second half is in development.
In progressA single form service that deploys on its own, replacing 32 legacy endpoints. Twelve are done, and the remaining gaps are mapped.
UnfundedMore was scoped but didn’t get funded.

Once the form service is finished, one team can own it, and the tech debt and upkeep of seven separate implementations go away with it. Forms also work across every area of the platform instead of living inside one product, so people can use a form wherever they’re working. Getting there meant working across four teams, and the system now has AI and MCP layers on top. The migration took two months of design research and six months of development, and the research kept going through the build. It’s going well so far: 79% moved across seamlessly, support tickets are down, and the same issues aren’t coming back.

Not all of it has shipped yet. It’s going out in stages, and I ordered them by what users ranked highest, so the first ones out are the ones the research backed most.

One thing I’d do differently

I fixed the builder before the front door
By round two, finding Forms was the only real friction left. I left navigation for later because the survey ranked parity features higher. I’d flip that now. The navigation fix was much cheaper than the features I shipped first.
