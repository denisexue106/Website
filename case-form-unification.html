TL;DR

Keap had 7 form builders doing the same job, and no single team owned them. I argued for merging them into one builder on the existing backend instead of a 12+ month rebuild. 01 · The problem

Seven ways to build a form, and users still left

Four live builders and three legacy ones, each with its own UX, spread across four teams. Any improvement had to be built seven times, and no single team owned the whole problem, so I took it on.

I thought web forms ARE public forms. Do not know the difference.Survey respondent

Users couldn’t tell them apart

The names came from our product vocabulary, not theirs, so there was no obvious one to move to.

So they built forms elsewhere

Our survey found four other form tools people used instead, and a quarter named the same one.

One form got worse

Only one form hit its measured target, and the most used form scored lower after its rebuild than before.

02 · My role

The only designer on it

01

Pulled six years of research together

Five studies since 2020 had each sat in a folder. Side by side, they showed the problem was structural.

02

Ran new research

A survey of 25 people with two researchers, an audit of nine competitors, a prioritization workshop, and both usability studies.

03

Set the direction and the phases

Parity (matching every feature the old builders had before adding new ones) first, then consolidation, then appointments and payments. I got the team that owned checkout on board first.

04

Argued against the full rebuild

Engineering estimated a rebuild at twelve months or more. I showed the data models were sound and the fragmentation was in what users saw, so a rebuild meant regression risk for little payoff.

03 · The approach

Ask what the form is for

One builder with four use cases replaces seven products, and the use case decides where the form can appear.

04 · Testing

Round one went badly, and that was useful

Round one · 7 participants

None of the seven could add a step, and six got tripped up by the old categories. The live preview earned their trust.

Round two · 8 participants

A year later, manual creation scored a perfect 3.00 on the flow that broke for everyone, and most people didn’t notice the merge.

What still didn’t work

Finding Forms was the only real friction left. People looked in Automations, Contacts, Sales and Reporting first.

05 · AI

One builder made AI drafts possible

With one builder instead of seven, AI can produce a complete form that uses everything the builder supports.

Fields, copy, brand and template arrive together. Interactive prototype.

I won’t use AI for minor edits: typing instructions takes longer than just doing it. But I would for an entirely new form.Usability study participant

Nearly everyone tries AI once, so I added suggestion chips and templates so nobody starts from an empty prompt box.

06 · Mobile

Built on a phone, filled on a phone

I designed both sides on a phone, the owner who makes the form and the customer who fills it out, with the same builder and Thryv AI as desktop.

Tap, don’t type

Answers you can tap, so a draft can come from two taps instead of a paragraph.

Fields sized for fingers

52 pixel fields with 16 pixel text, so iOS doesn’t zoom in. Submit stays pinned in reach.

07 · Decisions

Where I changed direction

The full rebuild
I kept the existing backend, merged the UI on top of it, and shipped it in stages.
Merging all form types at once
The backend was still split, and checkout had its own payment logic. I merged the two closest first and designed the rest as future states.
Full AI form creation
People wanted AI to start a form, not for small edits. I shipped AI drafting with manual editing after it, and templates as the fallback.
Selling it with a deck
Slides couldn’t show what a three year plan would feel like. I built a working prototype instead. Leadership got it within days, and I got the sign off.

A Kano study (a survey method that sorts features by how much users notice their presence or absence) didn’t match our plan. Conditional logic ranked highest in both it and the survey, but wasn’t on the roadmap. I took that to leadership: conditional logic moved up, and the payments phase came out.

Who decided what

Me, as de facto PM
I made the scope and product calls: merge instead of rebuild, what parity covered, and the AI direction.
The PM and PO
Owned the timeline, delivery and story writing.
Leadership
Signed off on the roadmap change.
08 · Outcome

What shipped and what’s next

ShippedAll three of the survey’s top ranked parity features.
ShippedThe first half of the unified builder. The second half is in development.
In progressA single form service replacing 32 legacy endpoints (old backend APIs the previous builders depended on). Twelve are done, and the remaining gaps are mapped.
UnfundedMore was scoped but not funded.

Once the form service is finished, one team can own it, and the tech debt of seven implementations goes away. Forms also work across the platform, with AI and MCP (Model Context Protocol, a standard way to let AI tools call the product’s functions) layers on top. It took four teams, two months of design research and six months of development. So far, 79% of about 100K form users created at least one form in the new builder within three months of launch, and support reported fewer form-builder tickets after launch.

One thing I’d do differently

I fixed the builder before the front door
I put navigation off because the survey ranked parity features higher. I’d flip that now. The navigation fix was much cheaper than the features I shipped first.
