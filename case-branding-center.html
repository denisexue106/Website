Building the
Branding Center

A business’s brand used to live in a document outside the product, so changing a logo meant redoing every email and landing page by hand. I designed one place to keep the brand, and everything else reads from it. It covers four parts of the product and sixteen teams.

01 · The problem

Changing a logo took hours, sometimes weeks

Partners build campaigns for their clients: fifteen or more emails and landing pages in a single campaign. When a client sent a new logo or changed a button color, the partner opened every asset in every automation and redid it by hand.

No way to apply a brand

There was no good way to apply a brand to emails and landing pages, so old assets and the wrong logos kept going out.

Hours to weeks, per client

Partners told us the workaround took anywhere from a few hours to a few weeks for a single client app.

Shared content arrived unbranded

Campaigns shared into a client’s own app showed up with none of that client’s branding.

02 · My role

What I owned

I owned the research and the information architecture, and carried the design across four teams and a builder we don’t own.

01

Ran the research

Thirteen one to one sessions of 45 minutes with partners, onboarding coaches and end users. After that, a competitor audit of five platforms and a concept test before anything got built.

02

Set the rules

Where brand assets live, what a “primary” logo means, and how one definition reaches every surface that renders it.

03

Worked across sixteen teams

I pitched connecting the Branding Center to a new landing page builder, and the pitch listed sixteen teams that would depend on it. My two teams built it, and those sixteen use it with our support. My job was keeping one brand definition working for all of them without owning any of them.

04

Ran the cross team workshop

I ran a workshop with the PM, tech leads, engineers and the content sharing team: current state, future vision, then two rounds of sketching on the same board.

03 · The rules

Three rules every surface follows

It looked like a settings page, but the hard part was getting one brand to show up correctly everywhere. So I wrote down three rules that had to hold on every surface, no matter who built it.

Reference, never copy

Surfaces point to the brand asset instead of keeping their own copy, so one change updates it everywhere.

Exactly one primary

One logo is marked primary. That’s the one that goes along when content is shared into someone else’s app.

Degrade to a placeholder

If an asset is missing, the surface shows a placeholder, never another business’s brand.

I also designed for what accounts would really look like on day one, not just the happy path: accounts with no logo, and accounts where the existing profile logo didn’t match the new one. That second case is the one that turns into support tickets.

04 · Key decisions

Why I pushed for the slower build

Engineering proposed universal keys with a dependency mapping table instead of raw IDs. It was the slower build, and I pushed for it anyway, because I could show that sharing would need to expand later and raw IDs would have ruled that out. I won that argument on design grounds.

Marketing or Settings?
In testing, people looked under Marketing first but found it in Settings without much trouble. I chose Settings. It’s a little harder to find, but there’s only one place the brand lives.
One surface or three tabs?
Splitting Branding from a Style Guide confused people about where logos, colors and fonts lived. I tested the two structures against each other. I went with one place, because people could guess where things were.
Custom fonts in email?
Most email clients don’t show them at all. I said no and locked the option in the email builder, with a note explaining why, instead of letting people pick a font that would never show up.
How many assets?
Every extra asset is one more thing every surface has to handle. I set a cap on purpose, with a banner that warns people before they reach it.
05 · Outcome

What shipped, and what the usage data showed

25→15
Minutes to build a landing page, and 15→10 for an email
+20%
Landing page builder adoption after it shipped
3:1
Logos uploaded for every color, the reverse of what discovery predicted
ShippedColors and logos, reusable across emails and landing pages. I had every account’s existing logo added as its primary, so nobody started with an empty page.
ShippedShared content support. When content is installed in another app, it picks up that app’s primary logo, or a placeholder if there isn’t one.
ShippedCustom fonts, rolled out in stages. Uploads are scanned for malware, and a quarantined font switches to the fallback on its own.
UnfundedMore was scoped but didn’t get funded. What shipped is what the usage data could justify.
CancelledMass logo update. Research pointed right at it and I designed it fully, but the project was cancelled because of funding and complications.

Adoption of the landing page builder rose 20% once brand assets became reusable inside it. Sixteen teams still build on the Branding Center today.

Discovery told me colors mattered most and logos second. Six months after launch, people had uploaded three logos for every color. I took that into my next project: get the baseline numbers before drawing anything.
